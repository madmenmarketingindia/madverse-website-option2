The decisions
that define what
a business becomes next.

Growth changes the questions a business must answer. Strong leadership means recognising which decisions will shape everything that follows.

A business is shaped less by the number of decisions its leaders make than by whether they recognise the few decisions that change the path of everything else.

01 · The consequential few

Not every decision deserves equal leadership attention.

As organisations grow, decision volume rises quickly. New markets, new hires, new services and new expectations create a constant stream of choices. The natural response is to move faster and become involved in more. But leadership stretched across every question eventually becomes a source of delay rather than clarity.

The real work is identifying the consequential few: decisions that establish a direction, concentrate resources or set a standard others will repeat. Who are we choosing to serve? What promise are we prepared to organise around? Which capability must become exceptional? What will we deliberately stop doing? Answers to these questions create the context for hundreds of smaller choices.

A consequential decision should not be judged only by the immediate outcome. It should be judged by the quality of decisions it enables afterward. When a choice gives teams a useful boundary, a clearer priority and a shared definition of good, its value compounds far beyond the meeting in which it was made.

This also requires separating reversible choices from identity-shaping ones. Many operational decisions can be made quickly, tested and corrected by the people closest to the work. Choices about positioning, culture, capability and capital deserve a different pace because they influence what the organisation will become good at—and what it may become unable to see. Speed is valuable, but only after the significance of the choice has been understood.

02 · Direction that travels

A decision is only strategic when the organisation can use it.

Leaders often mistake communication for transmission. A direction may be announced clearly and still fail to travel. Teams hear the conclusion without understanding the reasoning, so they cannot apply it when circumstances change. The organisation remains dependent on the people who made the original choice.

Direction travels when people understand the tension behind it: the opportunity being pursued, the trade-off being accepted and the evidence that would justify a different course. This gives specialists enough context to exercise judgement without seeking permission at every turn. Autonomy becomes aligned action rather than independent motion.

The strongest leaders therefore make decisions visible in the systems around them. Strategy appears in briefs, investment choices, hiring priorities, review criteria and the questions asked in weekly conversations. Repetition is not a failure of communication. It is how direction becomes part of the operating culture.

Shared context is especially important in a specialist organisation. Experts should not be reduced to executing a central answer; their value lies in seeing possibilities that leadership cannot see alone. A useful direction gives them a common problem and enough freedom to improve the answer through their craft. Alignment is not sameness. It is different expertise moving toward the same outcome.

None of this makes an important decision permanent. Good leadership defines what evidence will be watched, when the decision will be reviewed and who is responsible for interpreting what has changed. That creates learning without encouraging the organisation to relitigate its direction after every difficult week. Consistency and adaptability stop being opposites when the review rhythm is designed in advance.

“Leadership creates leverage when clarity continues working after the leader leaves the room.”

03 · Decision architecture

Build a business that can make better choices without waiting.

  1. 01

    Name the decisions only leadership can make, then protect time to frame them properly.

  2. 02

    Explain the opportunity, evidence and trade-offs behind a direction—not only the final answer.

  3. 03

    Translate strategic choices into practical principles teams can use when the situation is ambiguous.

  4. 04

    Make decision rights explicit so ownership sits with the people closest to the relevant knowledge.

  5. 05

    Review important decisions against new evidence without turning every review into a reset.
